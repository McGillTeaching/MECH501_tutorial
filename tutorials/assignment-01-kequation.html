Assignment 1 · Part B: The turbulent kinetic energy budget

Reynolds decomposition, TKE and dissipation from isotropic DNS

Author

MECH 501/600 — Computational Turbulence

Published

September 9, 2026

NoteDeliverables

1 What you will do

In the data-processing tutorial you learned to read the field and manipulate it. Here you use it to evaluate the two central quantities of any turbulence calculation — the turbulent kinetic energy \(k\) and its dissipation rate \(\varepsilon\) — directly from the DNS, and then build the characteristic scales of the flow from them. This mirrors the \(k\)-equation analysis from lecture: instead of modelling these terms, you measure them.

2 The turbulent kinetic energy equation

Applying the Reynolds decomposition \(u_i = \langle u_i\rangle + u_i'\) to the Navier–Stokes equations and taking the mean gives the transport equation for the turbulent kinetic energy \(k = \tfrac12\langle u_i' u_i'\rangle\):

\[ \underbrace{\frac{\partial k}{\partial t}}_{\text{unsteady}} + \underbrace{\langle u_j\rangle \frac{\partial k}{\partial x_j}}_{\text{advection}} = \underbrace{-\langle u_i' u_j'\rangle \frac{\partial \langle u_i\rangle}{\partial x_j}}_{\text{production } \mathcal{P}} - \underbrace{\varepsilon}_{\text{dissipation}} + \underbrace{\frac{\partial}{\partial x_j}\!\left(\cdots\right)}_{\text{transport } \mathcal{T}} \tag{1}\]

with the dissipation rate

\[ \varepsilon = 2\nu \langle s_{ij}' s_{ij}'\rangle, \qquad s_{ij}' = \tfrac12\!\left(\frac{\partial u_i'}{\partial x_j} + \frac{\partial u_j'}{\partial x_i}\right). \tag{2}\]

TipWhy the budget collapses for our flow

Forced homogeneous isotropic turbulence is the cleanest possible case for Eq. 1, because almost every term drops out:

  • No mean shear. \(\langle u_i\rangle \approx 0\) everywhere, so the mean advection term and the production term \(\mathcal{P} = -\langle u_i' u_j'\rangle\,\partial_j\langle u_i\rangle\) both vanish — there is no mean gradient to extract energy from.
  • Homogeneity. Statistics do not depend on position, so every spatial divergence — the transport term \(\mathcal{T}\) — averages to zero.
  • Stationarity. The forcing injects energy at the large scales at a mean rate \(\mathcal{F}\) that, on average, holds \(\partial k/\partial t = 0\).

What remains is a single statement: injection balances dissipation, \(\mathcal{F} = \varepsilon\). You cannot measure the artificial forcing \(\mathcal{F}\) from a snapshot, but you can measure \(k\) and \(\varepsilon\) directly — and from them every characteristic scale of the flow. That is the task.

3 Reading the field as a structured cube

The tasks below assume the field has been reshaped from the flat table into a \(200^3\) NumPy array per velocity component. The grid spacing is that of the parent \(1024^3\) simulation, \(dx = 2\pi/1024\), so the sub-cube has side \(L = 200\,dx = 2\pi\,(200/1024)\).

import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

nu = 1.85e-4                       # kinematic viscosity of the JHTDB isotropic DNS
dx = 2 * np.pi / 1024              # grid spacing of the parent 1024^3 simulation
dim = 200                          # our sub-cube is 200^3

df = pd.read_parquet("/project/60004/00_Assignment1/isotropic.parquet")

def to_cube(component):
    """Scatter a flat velocity column onto the structured 200^3 grid."""
    cube = np.zeros((dim, dim, dim), dtype=np.float64)
    ix = (df["x"] / dx).round().astype(int)
    iy = (df["y"] / dx).round().astype(int)
    iz = (df["z"] / dx).round().astype(int)
    cube[ix, iy, iz] = df[component].values
    return cube

u = to_cube("velocity_x")
v = to_cube("velocity_y")
w = to_cube("velocity_z")
print(u.shape, u.dtype)

4 Task 1 — Reynolds decomposition

ImportantTask 1

Because the flow is homogeneous, the ensemble average of lecture is, by ergodicity, the spatial average over the cube. Compute the mean of each velocity component, \(\langle u_i\rangle\), and confirm it is small compared to the fluctuations. Then form the fluctuations \(u_i' = u_i - \langle u_i\rangle\) and compute the three component variances \(\langle u_i'^2\rangle\).

Report the three variances and comment (1–2 sentences): are they approximately equal, and what does that tell you about isotropy?

5 Task 2 — Turbulent kinetic energy

ImportantTask 2

Compute the turbulent kinetic energy

\[ k = \tfrac12\langle u_i' u_i'\rangle = \tfrac12\!\left(\langle u_1'^2\rangle + \langle u_2'^2\rangle + \langle u_3'^2\rangle\right). \]

Report \(k\) and the equivalent single-component rms velocity \(u_{\mathrm{rms}} = \sqrt{2k/3}\).

6 Task 3 — Dissipation rate

ImportantTask 3

Evaluate the dissipation rate from its definition, Eq. 2. Use numpy.gradient with spacing dx to obtain the nine fluctuating velocity gradients \(\partial u_i'/\partial x_j\), build the fluctuating strain-rate tensor \(s_{ij}'\), and average:

\[ \varepsilon = 2\nu\,\langle s_{ij}' s_{ij}'\rangle . \]

Then compute the isotropic surrogate \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{iso}} = 15\,\nu\,\big\langle (\partial u_1'/\partial x_1)^2\big\rangle\), which uses a single gradient, and compare the two. How close are they, and why should they agree for this flow?

7 Task 4 — Characteristic scales

ImportantTask 4

From \(k\), \(\varepsilon\) and \(\nu\) alone, compute:

  • the Kolmogorov scales \(\eta = (\nu^3/\varepsilon)^{1/4}\), \(\tau_\eta = (\nu/\varepsilon)^{1/2}\), \(u_\eta = (\nu\varepsilon)^{1/4}\);
  • the Taylor microscale \(\lambda = \sqrt{10\,\nu k/\varepsilon}\);
  • the Taylor-microscale Reynolds number \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda = \dfrac{u_{\mathrm{rms}}\,\lambda}{\nu} = \sqrt{\dfrac{20\,k^2}{3\,\nu\,\varepsilon}}\).

Compare your \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda\) to the published value for this dataset, \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda \approx 433\). Do they agree?

8 Task 5 — Resolution and the budget

ImportantTask 5

Two short written answers, backed by a number each:

  1. Resolution. The largest resolved wavenumber is \(\kappa_{\max} = \pi/dx\). Compute \(\kappa_{\max}\eta\). A well-resolved DNS has \(\kappa_{\max}\eta \gtrsim 1\). Is this field well resolved at the dissipative scale?
  2. The budget. Using Eq. 1 and the argument in the callout above, explain in 2–3 sentences why, for this flow, your measured \(\varepsilon\) is also (in the mean) the rate at which the forcing injects energy — even though you never computed the forcing.
