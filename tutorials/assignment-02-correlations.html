Assignment 2a: Two-point correlations & length scales

Verifying isotropy relations on the JHTDB DNS cube

Author

MECH 501/600 — Computational Turbulence

Published

September 22, 2026

NoteWhat this assignment is about

This assignment ties together the two ways we characterise a turbulent flow, both on Johns Hopkins isotropic DNS data. First you measure the flow’s integral quantities directly from the field — the turbulent kinetic energy \(k\), the dissipation rate \(\varepsilon\), and the characteristic scales built from them — on the full-resolution \(200^3\) sub-cube from Tutorial 1 (the small scales must be resolved for \(\varepsilon\)). Then you turn to the two-point correlation: you verify two classical isotropy results on a larger \(512^3\) periodic box (a fresh download that samples the large-scale correlations far better), reproducing the longitudinal and transverse autocorrelations \(f(r)\) and \(g(r)\) (the classic grid-turbulence measurement) and the integral length scales.

The turbulent kinetic energy budget

In the Assignment 1 overview you learned to read the isotropic DNS field and manipulate it. Here you use it to evaluate the two central quantities of any turbulence calculation — the turbulent kinetic energy \(k\) and its dissipation rate \(\varepsilon\) — directly from the DNS, and then build the characteristic scales of the flow from them. This mirrors the \(k\)-equation analysis from lecture: instead of modelling these terms, you measure them.

The turbulent kinetic energy equation

Applying the Reynolds decomposition \(u_i = \langle u_i\rangle + u_i'\) to the Navier–Stokes equations and taking the mean gives the transport equation for the turbulent kinetic energy \(k = \tfrac12\langle u_i' u_i'\rangle\):

\[ \underbrace{\frac{\partial k}{\partial t}}_{\text{unsteady}} + \underbrace{\langle u_j\rangle \frac{\partial k}{\partial x_j}}_{\text{advection}} = \underbrace{-\langle u_i' u_j'\rangle \frac{\partial \langle u_i\rangle}{\partial x_j}}_{\text{production } \mathcal{P}} - \underbrace{\varepsilon}_{\text{dissipation}} + \underbrace{\frac{\partial}{\partial x_j}\!\left(\cdots\right)}_{\text{transport } \mathcal{T}} \tag{1}\]

with the dissipation rate

\[ \varepsilon = 2\nu \langle s_{ij}' s_{ij}'\rangle, \qquad s_{ij}' = \tfrac12\!\left(\frac{\partial u_i'}{\partial x_j} + \frac{\partial u_j'}{\partial x_i}\right). \tag{2}\]

TipWhy the budget collapses for our flow

Forced homogeneous isotropic turbulence is the cleanest possible case for Eq. 1, because almost every term drops out:

  • No mean shear. \(\langle u_i\rangle \approx 0\) everywhere, so the mean advection term and the production term \(\mathcal{P} = -\langle u_i' u_j'\rangle\,\partial_j\langle u_i\rangle\) both vanish — there is no mean gradient to extract energy from.
  • Homogeneity. Statistics do not depend on position, so every spatial divergence — the transport term \(\mathcal{T}\) — averages to zero.
  • Stationarity. The forcing injects energy at the large scales at a mean rate \(\mathcal{F}\) that, on average, holds \(\partial k/\partial t = 0\).

What remains is a single statement: injection balances dissipation, \(\mathcal{F} = \varepsilon\). You cannot measure the artificial forcing \(\mathcal{F}\) from a snapshot, but you can measure \(k\) and \(\varepsilon\) directly — and from them every characteristic scale of the flow.

Loading the cube

The tasks below assume the field has been reshaped from the flat table into a \(200^3\) NumPy array per velocity component, exactly as in Tutorial 1. The grid spacing is that of the parent \(1024^3\) simulation, \(dx = 2\pi/1024\). This load serves the budget part (Tasks 1–4); Part (c) loads a separate, larger cube. These files store the velocity components relative to the coordinate axes, so the loader also aligns them (a component’s longitudinal gradient is the smallest of its three) — this matters for both the dissipation surrogate \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{iso}}\) and the longitudinal/transverse correlations below.

import numpy as np, pandas as pd, matplotlib.pyplot as plt

nu  = 1.85e-4                            # kinematic viscosity of the JHTDB isotropic DNS
dim = 200                                # our sub-cube is 200^3
dx  = 2*np.pi/1024                       # grid spacing of the parent 1024^3 DNS

df  = pd.read_parquet('/project/60004/00_Assignment1/isotropic_t1.parquet')
ii  = lambda c: (df[c]/dx).round().astype(int)

raw = []
for col in ('velocity_x', 'velocity_y', 'velocity_z'):
    a = np.zeros((dim,)*3, np.float64); a[ii('x'),ii('y'),ii('z')] = df[col].values
    raw.append(a)

# align components with the axes: a component's longitudinal (parallel) gradient
# is the smallest, so each component's own axis = argmin of its mean-square gradients
g = [np.gradient(a - a.mean(), dx, dx, dx) for a in raw]
G = np.array([[(g[i][j]**2).mean() for j in range(3)] for i in range(3)])
long_axis = [int(np.argmin(G[i])) for i in range(3)]
assert sorted(long_axis) == [0, 1, 2], "component-axis alignment ambiguous"
u, v, w = (raw[long_axis.index(k)] for k in range(3))
print("aligned (x,y,z) = components", [long_axis.index(k) for k in range(3)], u.shape)

Task 3 — Dissipation rate

ImportantTask 3

Evaluate the dissipation rate from its definition, Eq. 2. Use numpy.gradient with spacing dx to obtain the nine fluctuating velocity gradients \(\partial u_i'/\partial x_j\), build the fluctuating strain-rate tensor \(s_{ij}'\), and average:

\[ \varepsilon = 2\nu\,\langle s_{ij}' s_{ij}'\rangle . \]

Then compute the isotropic surrogate \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{iso}} = 15\,\nu\,\big\langle (\partial u_1'/\partial x_1)^2\big\rangle\), which uses a single gradient, and compare the two. How close are they, and why should they agree for this flow?

Task 4 — Characteristic scales

ImportantTask 4

From \(k\), \(\varepsilon\) and \(\nu\) alone, compute:

  • the Kolmogorov scales \(\eta = (\nu^3/\varepsilon)^{1/4}\), \(\tau_\eta = (\nu/\varepsilon)^{1/2}\), \(u_\eta = (\nu\varepsilon)^{1/4}\);
  • the Taylor microscale \(\lambda = \sqrt{10\,\nu k/\varepsilon}\);
  • the Taylor-microscale Reynolds number \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda = \dfrac{u_{\mathrm{rms}}\,\lambda}{\nu} = \sqrt{\dfrac{20\,k^2}{3\,\nu\,\varepsilon}}\).

Report your measured \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda\). (For these files it comes out of order \(280\) — averaging the four snapshots \(t_1\)–\(t_4\) tightens it — and it is the \(433\) of the standard dataset.)

Two-point correlations and length scales

Background

For homogeneous isotropic turbulence the two-point correlation \[ R_{ij}(\mathbf r)=\langle u_i'(\mathbf x+\mathbf r)\,u_j'(\mathbf x)\rangle \] is completely determined by two scalar functions of the separation distance — the longitudinal and transverse autocorrelations, \[ f(r)=\frac{\langle u_1'(\mathbf x+r\mathbf e_1)\,u_1'(\mathbf x)\rangle}{\langle u_1'^2\rangle}, \qquad g(r)=\frac{\langle u_2'(\mathbf x+r\mathbf e_1)\,u_2'(\mathbf x)\rangle}{\langle u_2'^2\rangle}, \] with \(f(0)=g(0)=1\). Continuity (\(\partial R_{ij}/\partial r_j=0\)) links the two: \[ g(r)=f(r)+\tfrac12\,r\,\frac{\partial f}{\partial r}. \tag{$\star$} \] The integral length scales are the areas under the curves, \[ L_{11}=\int_0^\infty f(r)\,dr,\qquad L_{22}=\int_0^\infty g(r)\,dr . \]

Two classical consequences of \((\star)\), which you will verify on the data below, are that the transverse integral scale is half the longitudinal one, \(L_{22}=\tfrac12 L_{11}\), and that the transverse correlation has zero first moment, \(\int_0^\infty r\,g\,dr=0\) — so \(g\) must dip negative at intermediate \(r\).

Now measure \(f\), \(g\) and the length scales on the real cube and check both. Because HIT has no mean flow, the fluctuations are the whole signal (\(u_i'\approx u_i\)).

c.1 Load the box and prepare the fields

The correlations use the 512³ periodic ensemble (isotropic_512_ens*.npy — the full \(2\pi\) box sampled at stride 2), a much larger box than the budget sub-cube, so the large-scale correlations are far better resolved. Load one snapshot, split it into fluctuation components (HIT has no mean flow, so \(u_i'\approx u_i\)), and set up the separation axis \(r\):

import glob
dim = 512                                # 512^3 full periodic box
dx  = 2*np.pi/dim                         # stride-2 grid spacing
FILES = sorted(glob.glob('/project/60004/00_Assignment1/isotropic_512_ens*.npy'))

arr = np.load(FILES[0]).astype(np.float32)   # (512, 512, 512, 3): components on the last axis
u = arr[..., 0] - arr[..., 0].mean()         # fluctuations (raw JHTDB is already x,y,z aligned)
v = arr[..., 1] - arr[..., 1].mean()
w = arr[..., 2] - arr[..., 2].mean()
del arr                                       # free ~1.6 GB

r = np.arange(dim//2)*dx                       # separation axis

c.2 An autocorrelation helper (FFT)

The two-point correlation along one axis is an autocorrelation, so the FFT (Wiener–Khinchin theorem) computes it in one shot; average over the two perpendicular directions to reduce the noise:

def corr_axis(field, axis):
    """Normalized autocorrelation of `field` along `axis`, averaged over the plane."""
    F = np.fft.fft(field, axis=axis)
    R = np.fft.ifft((F*np.conj(F)).real, axis=axis).real        # Wiener–Khinchin
    R = R.mean(axis=tuple(k for k in range(3) if k != axis))    # average other two axes
    return R[:field.shape[axis]//2] / R[0]                      # nonneg lags, R(0)=1

c.3 Your own correlation, then trust the FFT

The FFT helper is a black box until you have reproduced it yourself. Implement the two-point correlation directly from its definition and check that the two agree.

Along one axis, the normalized correlation at integer lag \(m\) is

\[ R(m)=\frac{\big\langle\, u(\mathbf{x})\,u(\mathbf{x}+m\,\mathbf{e})\,\big\rangle_{\mathbf{x}}} {\big\langle\, u(\mathbf{x})^2\,\big\rangle_{\mathbf{x}}}, \]

the product of the field with a copy of itself shifted by \(m\) cells, averaged over all starting points. The DNS box is periodic, so use a circular shift (np.roll) — that is exactly the correlation the FFT helper returns via Wiener–Khinchin, so the two must match to round-off.

NoteExpected shapes (sanity check)

\(f(r)\) starts at 1 and decays monotonically; \(g(r)\) starts at 1, decays faster, and dips slightly negative before returning to zero — that negative lobe is exactly what makes \(\int_0^\infty r\,g\,dr = 0\). The integral-scale ratio should land near \(L_{22}/L_{11}\approx0.5\). Small deviations are finite-box and finite-sample effects.
